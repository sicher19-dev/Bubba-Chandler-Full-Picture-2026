The storylines

Arm slot — up over the winter, down through the summer

He finished 2025 in Pittsburgh at a 43.7° arm angle. He opened 2026 higher, 50.8° across April, peaking at 54.3° on May 10. From June it came down, and July through September averaged 42.1°, 41.1° and 42.2° — below where he finished last year. The fastball's shape moved with the slot: ride went from 18.6 inches in April to 16.3 in August, and arm-side run from 6.6 to 10.1.

Slider — harder from April to August, and it stopped missing bats

In April the slider sat at 89.5 mph with 1.5 inches of ride. By August it was 93.0 mph with 5.9 inches, and September's whiff rate on it was 8%, from 33% in May and 35% in June. It turned into a cutter. The flat, one-inch version is the one that missed bats.

Mix — the April arsenal didn't last

The sweeper was 13% of his pitches in April and had disappeared by September, despite a 29% whiff rate. The changeup fell from 20% to 9%. The sinker came in at the end of April and ran 10% of his pitches from May on. It was no better on contact than the four-seam (.365 xwOBA against .358) and drew fewer whiffs (14% against 22%), so dropping it costs him little the data can see.

Velocity and extension — steady until September

The four-seam averaged 98.4 mph in April and stayed within half a mile an hour of that through August. September dropped to 97.4, and his last start of the year, Sep 25, was his slowest at 96.3. Extension stayed between 6.54 and 6.82 ft every month. Nothing in this channel points at the slot change.

This is the channel that moved most. He came into 2026 with a higher, more over-the-top arm slot than he finished 2025 with, held it for two months, and then lost it through the summer until he was lower than he'd been in 2025.

The slot drives the fastball's shape

Start by start in 2026, arm angle tracks the fastball's shape closely: r = 0.79 with ride and r = −0.79 with arm-side run.

Higher slot, more ride and less run. Lower slot, the reverse. It explains nothing about velocity (r = 0.09), which is why the velocity chart barely moves while the shape chart does. The fastball he threw in April (18.6 inches of ride, 6.6 of run) and the one he threw in August (16.3 and 10.1) are different pitches coming out of the same arm speed.

What the slot did and did not do

The higher-slot stretch was not clearly the better one. Through June 7 (arm angle 50.0°) contact against the four-seam came in at a .365 xwOBA; from June 13 on (42.5°) it was .352, about the same. He struck out more hitters in the high-slot stretch (22% against 20%) but also walked more (14% against 10%).

More ride in April did not buy better contact on the fastball. The target for the winter is a slot he can repeat, not a return to April.

That is the question for the motion capture. The April slot came with the release moving toward the middle of the rubber; the summer slot came with it moving back out. Whether that is the leg pick-up, the direction of the stride, or trunk tilt is what the mocap can separate — and whether the April version or the summer version is the one he can hold for 30 starts.

The leg pick-up change didn't reset the slot

The week before his August 3 start in Milwaukee he worked on his leg pick-up. That night's arm angle was 41.2°, against 42.7° across the five starts before, and release height was 5.64 ft against 5.72. That's the same downward drift that was already underway in July, not a break from it. Whatever the leg change did, it didn't reset where the ball came out.

This is the steady part of his year. Velocity and extension came out of the winter where he left them and held until September.

The slider became a cutter

Through June the slider sat around 89.8 mph with 2.5 inches of ride. From late July it sat at 92.4 mph with 6.1. Its spin tilt moved toward a fastball's too — 128° in the first half against 197° from late July, where a four-seam sits at 213°. It was being thrown more like a fastball, and it played like one.

The changeup changed in August

Through July the changeup was 91.7 mph with 2,067 rpm and 7.1 inches of ride. From mid-August it came in at 89.9 mph, 1,346 rpm and 1.8 inches — a real shape change, in line with the grip work in August: more drop and a bigger gap off the fastball. That's 64 pitches: 30% whiffs and a .251 xwOBA on contact, against 25% and .307 before. Too few to credit either way. It stays open until it faces hitters in spring.

How the mix moved

The sweeper was 13% of his pitches in April and was last thrown on Aug 3, 2026. In 108 pitches it drew a 29% whiff rate, the best of any pitch he threw regularly, with only 11 balls put in play. That's a small sample, but nothing in it argues against keeping the pitch in his back pocket.

The sinker arrived at the end of April. In 218 pitches it drew 14% whiffs, the fewest of anything he threw, and a .365 xwOBA on contact, a shade worse than the four-seam. Dropping it costs him little the data can see.

Conventions

Release side and height are measured at the true release point, re-planed from the fixed tracking plane for Triple-A outings so every context sits on the same basis; that matched the big-league values to within 0.02 ft on every start checked. Release side and horizontal break are signed so that positive is toward his arm side. Pitch types follow one classification throughout. Arm angle is only available for big-league regular-season outings. xwOBA is on balls in play and home runs, with strikeouts and walks excluded; whiff rate is whiffs per swing.

Spring training and Triple-A outings use game pitch tracking from those parks; three 2025 spring outings carried 47 tracked pitches in total, so that row is thin. The 2025 big-league figures cover 461 pitches over seven outings and should be read with that in mind. No offseason bullpen sessions are included. If his winter bullpens were tracked, adding them would show where the slot actually changed.
